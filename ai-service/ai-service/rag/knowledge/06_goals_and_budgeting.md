# Goal Planning and Budgeting

## Setting a goal properly
A usable goal has four parts: a target amount, a deadline, a current saved
amount, and a monthly contribution that actually reaches the target.

Monthly requirement, ignoring returns:
    monthly = (target - already_saved) / months_remaining

With returns, use the SIP future value formula:
    FV = P x [((1 + r)^n - 1) / r] x (1 + r)
where P is the monthly amount, r is the monthly rate (annual / 12), n is months.

Example: Rs 5 lakh in 3 years. Plain saving needs Rs 13,889 a month. At an 8%
debt-fund return it needs roughly Rs 12,300 a month. Over short horizons returns
help only a little - the contribution does the work. Over 15 years the ratio
flips and returns contribute more than the contributions.

## Matching the instrument to the goal
- Goal within 1 year: savings account or liquid fund. Never equity.
- 1-3 years: RD, FD or short-duration debt fund.
- 3-5 years: hybrid or balanced advantage fund, some debt.
- 5+ years: equity SIP in index and flexi cap funds.
- Retirement: EPF, VPF, NPS and equity SIPs.
- Child's education: start with equity for the long stretch, then shift to debt
  in the final 2-3 years so a market fall right before the fee deadline cannot
  hurt you.

## Glide path
As a goal's deadline approaches, move money from equity to debt. A common
approach: fully invested in equity until 3 years out, then shift roughly a third
of the corpus to debt each year. This protects gains from a badly timed crash.

## Budgeting frameworks
- **50/30/20**: 50% needs, 30% wants, 20% savings. A reasonable starting point.
- **Zero-based**: every rupee is assigned a job at the start of the month.
  Effective but demanding.
- **Pay yourself first**: automate savings on salary day and spend the rest.
  The most reliable method because it removes willpower from the loop.

Benchmarks: a savings rate above 20% is healthy, above 30% is strong. Essential
expenses under 50% of take-home pay, total EMIs under 40%, rent under 30%.

## Prioritising several goals at once
1. Emergency fund
2. Insurance premiums
3. High-interest debt
4. Time-bound essential goals (education fees, a planned medical expense)
5. Retirement - never sacrificed for children's education, since education can be
   funded with a loan and retirement cannot
6. Lifestyle goals (travel, vehicle upgrade)

If the surplus cannot fund every goal, the honest answer is to extend a deadline,
lower a target, or increase income - not to chase higher returns with more risk.

## Diagnosing why a goal is off track
- The monthly contribution was never enough for the deadline - recompute it.
- Contributions are irregular - automate them.
- The instrument is wrong for the horizon - equity for a 1-year goal, or a savings
  account for a 15-year goal.
- Money keeps getting withdrawn for other things - the goal needs its own account.

## Tracking
Review goals monthly and ask three questions: is the contribution actually
happening, is the projected corpus still on track for the deadline, and has the
target amount changed because of inflation? For goals more than 5 years out,
inflate the target by around 6% a year - Rs 10 lakh needed in 10 years means
about Rs 18 lakh in future rupees. Education inflation runs higher, around 8-10%.
